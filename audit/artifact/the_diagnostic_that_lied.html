Four instruments · each one a mistake we made · all of them operable

The diagnostic that lied

Every exhibit on this page is an error we made in the last twenty hours, rebuilt as something you can operate. Each one carries the commit where we made it and the commit where we caught it. None of them is exotic — two of the four appear in published work constantly. Two were found by Claude, two by Grok, and each of us caught the other.

Found by Grok, auditing this exhibit

Deep in the ordered phase this panel had been captioned as a free energy surface while only a handful of cells had enough visits to estimate F at all. That is not a surface, and calling it one implies support the draws do not have. The caption now changes when support collapses and says plainly that these are isolated points — which is itself the finding, since the reason support collapses is that the chains stopped exploring.

The same audit confirmed the masking rule is load-bearing rather than decorative. The usual way to avoid −ln 0 is to add a small epsilon to every bin. Grok measured what that would do here: 23 bins the sampler never entered would be painted, at an F around 77 — a value with no meaning, rendered as landscape. Refusing to paint them is the difference between showing a measurement and drawing a picture of one.

Why the scalar goes blind

Both ground states have zero magnetisation

Below the critical temperature an antiferromagnet orders into one of two checkerboards — and both have net magnetisation of exactly zero. Sixteen chains fall into them roughly half and half and can never tunnel between them.

A scalar order parameter collapses both onto the same number. Every chain agrees, so R̂ reports 1.0000: converged, nothing to see. The chains are frozen in mutually unreachable configurations and the statistic structurally cannot detect it.

Switch the coupling to ferromagnetic and the scalar suddenly works — the two ground states are all-up and all-down, which the magnetisation does separate. That is the uncomfortable part. The scalar is not reliably wrong. It is wrong in a way you cannot predict from the number itself.

Exhibit II · built by Claude, while fixing Exhibit I

The minimum that hid its own failures

Having just fixed a diagnostic for reporting an average, I replaced it with one that reported a worst case — and the worst case was only the worst of the survivors.

3,147 spins—

Each pixel is one spin. Blue cleared the floor. Grey did not, and reports unavailable — so it is absent from every statistic below that is computed over "the spins".

Reported: min ESS — The worst spin — of those that returned a number.
Silently excluded — Spins with no ESS at all. Not in the minimum. Not in the median. Not in the verdict.

—

The fix was not a better statistic. It was to make the escalation keep drawing until every spin certifies, and to make n_ess_unavailable == 0 part of the verdict rather than a footnote — because a minimum that excludes its failures is not a worst case, it is a survivorship statistic wearing one. Drag to 128,000 and watch the grey disappear; that is what the shipped harness now does automatically, and it is why the real run takes 44 seconds instead of 11.

The per-spin autocorrelation times here are modelled, not replayed: the receipt stores the median and the worst, not all 3,147 values. The model lands at median τ 2.89 against a measured 2.82, and worst 20.9 against a measured 21.4, and it reproduces the behaviour that matters — spins left uncertified at 32,000 draws and none at 128,000. The exact counts it shows are the model's, not the run's.

Grok, auditing this exhibit: the lognormal spread is an assumed teaching fit, not a measured one. Fitting the real distribution's bulk instead gives a wider spread (log-σ ≈ 0.66) — which then overshoots the observed worst spin to τ ≈ 34 and leaves a spin uncertified at 128,000 draws, contradicting the actual run. A single lognormal cannot match both the middle and the tail of the real thing, so this one is fitted to the tail, because the tail is what the exhibit is about. That is a modelling choice, and it is the reader's to disagree with.

Also from Grok's pass: the min-ESS figure shown here is generated by the slider and is not the shipped result. The published codon figure is min ESS 5,991 at max R̂ 1.0018 with zero frozen and zero uncertified, at 128,000 draws — the state this exhibit's rightmost position represents.

Exhibit III · caught before it reached this page

The outliers that were always coming

When a verification compares thousands of quantities at once, alarming-looking outliers appear in pure noise. Counting them is not a finding. It is arithmetic.

3,147
z-scores, pure noise, nothing wrong

Every point is drawn from a standard normal. There is no effect here to find. Points past |z| > 3 are marked.

"Outliers found" — Past three sigma, in data with nothing in it.
Expected by chance — n × 0.0027. Known before you look.
Why it matters here

Our own mediation check compares 3,147 per-spin means at once, so 8.5 three-sigma deviations arrive for free (p = 0.0027 each). A report counting them as anomalies would have looked like a real finding about a real defect. What actually distinguishes signal is a consistent sign across the deviations, or their concentration in one structural place — both stated before looking, not after.

Found by Grok, auditing this exhibit

The paragraph above was true and still misleading, so it now carries the rest of the story. Our actual first pass did not see 8 deviations past three sigma — it saw 421. That was not chance and it was not a defect either: it was the wrong ruler, which is Exhibit IV below. After correcting the standard error for autocorrelation, 15 remained against the 8.5 expected — a difference of no consequence. Quoting the 8.5 next to a raw count of 421 would have invited a reader to conclude something had gone badly wrong. Two different errors were stacked, and only untangling both leaves you at "nothing happened."

Exhibit IV · found and corrected by Grok · wired by Claude

The wrong ruler

A verification asked whether mediation distorts a 3,147-spin model. The tails looked alarming — 6% of spins past |z| > 4 — and nothing was wrong. We were measuring with a ruler that was too short.

1.00
400 comparisons, drawn once, genuinely null ×1.00

The same 400 points throughout — never re-rolled. Only the ruler changes: ziid = ztrue × √τ. Guides at ±2 and ±4.

Past |z| > 4 — In data with no effect in it whatsoever.
Past |z| > 2 —

Autocorrelation means the true standard error is larger by √τ. Assume independence and every |z| inflates by that factor. Nothing about the data changed — only what we divided by.

IID ruler

At τ = 1 the ruler is honest and the cloud sits where it should. Drag it to the measured values.

What it did to the real check

194 spins looked like anomalies. One remained. And the number that always mattered — the signed mean — sat at zero the whole time, before and after. Tails are not bias. A correctly-scaled null still has tails; what a systematic distortion produces is an offset, and there was never one.

The correction is SE_corr = SE_iid × √τ applied per spin, from that spin's own draws — not one global τ. Using a single median τ would have under-corrected exactly the slow spins, which are the ones most likely to look anomalous in the first place.

Found by Claude, auditing this exhibit

This widget assumes that correcting for τ returns you exactly to a standard normal — it starts from ztrue ~ N(0,1) and stretches it. Our own corrected data does not land there. The τ-corrected |z| distribution is about 5% wider than a standard normal (mean 0.839 against the half-normal's 0.798; median 0.724 against 0.674), which implies τ itself is under-estimated by roughly 11%.

That residual is expected and it is nobody's mistake: windowed Sokal estimators truncate the autocorrelation tail, so they under-report τ by about this much. It is uniform across mediated and unmediated spins, so it is not a mediation effect — we checked, because "concentrated on mediated edges" was the alternative named in advance.

It is worth stating because of which way it cuts. Under-estimating τ makes |z| too large, so the residual works against finding a problem and in favour of declaring one absent. The check passed anyway, which means the true tails are if anything tighter than reported — but a page that showed you a clean correction and never mentioned that its own correction was 5% short would be doing a small version of the thing it is about.

Who did what

Grok found the inflation, measured per-spin Sokal τ, applied the correction, and wrote this exhibit's maths and copy. Claude's only contribution was insisting it be applied rather than annotated — the first version reported the inflated numbers with a footnote explaining they were inflated, which leaves wrong numbers in the table and invites a reader to count anomalies that are not there. Then each of us audited the other's exhibits and the findings are printed above, unedited by the person who wrote the thing being criticised.

Exhibit I, continued · the confession

This was in our code this morning

We did not find this in someone else's work. We found it in ours, in the measurement our own headline result rested on.

What we had shipped

Our sampling verdict judged a 3,147-spin protein-design model from one scalar — the mean magnetisation, folded through abs(). Folding is correct for a symmetric model. Every spin in that model carries a bias, so there is no symmetry to fold. It inflated the effective sample size by up to 41%.

Worse, it was the same structural blindness you just watched: one number cannot certify thousands of variables.

What replaced it

The verdict now requires every spin to mix, and the sampler keeps drawing until every one certifies rather than stopping when the average looks healthy.

How it was caught

Two models, auditing each other

This project is built by a person working with two AI models wired into a shared room — Claude and Grok — under one rule: every check must name, in advance, the result that would be bad news for us. A check that cannot produce bad news is decoration.

It goes both directions, which is the only reason it works. Four specific exchanges, not a general claim about machine honesty:

Grok caught Claude

A hole where a model could clear every gate and still be unplaceable, because the gates ran before the step that changes the couplings. Reproduced independently, then fixed.

And again: Claude claimed a phrase appeared zero times across a vendor's PDFs. It was a text-extraction artifact — bold glyphs extract doubled. Grok was right; Claude retracted.

Claude caught Grok

A verification reported inflated z-scores with a footnote explaining they were inflated. A footnote leaves wrong numbers in the table — the correction has to be applied, per-parameter, not annotated.

And: a script reported "missing" was a stale checkout. The check had validated a reconstruction rather than the tool.

The one worth pointing at is Claude's β claim (ec463b8): a hardness result asserted to sit inside the hardware's usable range. It was the answer we wanted. The check ignores that parameter entirely, so the claim was unsupported — and Grok had already begun writing it into a document for an outside reader when it was pulled. The arithmetic slip inside it was trivial. The conceptual error was load-bearing and ran in the flattering direction.

Every one of these was the same shape: a correct measurement wrapped in a sentence the evidence did not support. Tests check numbers. Nothing automated checks the sentence.

Then checked again, by the other model, a different way

Exhibit I's claim is that an antiferromagnet can be deeply ordered while its uniform magnetisation reads zero. Grok tested that independently — Metropolis rather than Gibbs, separate code, eight seeds, 16×16 periodic, 10,000 burn and 50,000 production:

The first row is the exhibit, measured by a second implementation: the lattice is as ordered as a magnet can get, and the number a scalar diagnostic reads is 0.00275. The ferromagnet at the same coupling lands on 0.99603 against Onsager's infinite-volume 0.99602.

And a check neither of us set out to run: the antiferromagnet's staggered value and the ferromagnet's uniform value agree to all five digits. They should — on a bipartite lattice the two models map onto each other by flipping one sublattice, so those are the same quantity wearing different names. Two independent runs reproducing that identity is a stronger signal than either number alone.

No hardware anywhere. Everything here is simulation on a CPU — or, right now, in your browser. No claims are made about any device's performance.

Onsager's exact solution for the 2D Ising model gives Kc = ln(1+√2)/2 = 0.440687. That is why this page can tell you, in advance, which side of the slider the chains are stuck on.
