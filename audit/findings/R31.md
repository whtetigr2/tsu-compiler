# R31 -- the load-bearing couplings are the ones carrying no correlation

**Status:** MEASURED, exactly. No sampling anywhere in this result, so nothing
in it depends on mixing.

Reproduce: `python audit/moment_readout_blindness.py`. Full run in
`out/moment-readout/run.txt`.

## The question

R30 showed two readouts of equal export cost disagreeing about a shipped
workload. It left the general version open, and named the nearest thing to a
theory: a Boltzmann machine on graph G is an exponential family whose minimal
sufficient statistics are the site values and the pair products on E(G).

That fact is usually read as reassurance. Export those moments and a chip has
exported everything, so moment readout is free. The reading is correct and the
reassurance does not follow, because the statement is indexed by G. Change the
graph and you change what "everything" means.

So: how much does the choice of G cost, and how does that compare to the
hardware limit people actually quote, the coupling cap?

## The setup

Bars-and-stripes on a 4x4 grid: the 30 configurations constant along every row
or constant along every column. 2^16 = 65536 states, so every distribution
below is enumerated rather than estimated.

For each candidate interaction graph, fit the maximum-entropy model whose
sufficient statistics are the site values and the pair products **on that
graph**. That model is exactly what a chip exporting those accumulators could
ever reconstruct. Then ask what it generates.

The fit minimises `log Z(t) - <t, target>`, which is convex with an exact
gradient, so L-BFGS converges without a step size to tune. A first attempt used
hand-tuned gradient ascent, diverged to a delta on the all-ones pattern, and
reported 0.9998 valid mass with moment errors near 1.0. That is a fit failure
wearing the costume of a result, and the script now gates every row on its
residual.

## The result

    graph                edges  deg   residual  KL(p||q)    valid  structured
    complete               120   15    1.5e-08     0.000   1.0000      0.9333
    8 neighbour             42    8    7.0e-08     0.000   1.0000      0.9333
    random (seed 0)         42    8    6.0e-08     3.591   0.0637      0.0408
    random (seed 2)         42    9    3.5e-08     5.111   0.0100      0.0082
    random (seed 1)         42    9    1.9e-08     5.632   0.0072      0.0048
    rook                    48    6    1.4e-13     4.694   0.1387      0.0078
    nearest neighbour       24    4    1.1e-12     5.295   0.0709      0.0143
    rows only               24    3    1.7e-10     5.848   0.0698      0.0611
    ring                    16    2    1.1e-12     6.321   0.0244      0.0221
    none (independent)       0    0    1.1e-16     7.689   0.0005      0.0004

Every row matched its own graph's moments to better than 1e-6, so each one is a
readout that saw everything it was given.

The maximum possible loss is 7.689 nats, the KL from the target to the uniform
distribution over all 65536 states.

**42 edges are enough to reconstruct bars-and-stripes exactly.** The
8-neighbour graph reaches KL 0 with a third of the complete graph's edges,
leaking 2.7e-13 of its mass outside the target's support and spreading the rest
across the 30 valid states to within 1.4e-08. That is an exact reconstruction
with optimiser noise on it, not a good approximation.

**The same 42 edges chosen differently lose almost everything.** Three random
draws at the identical budget land at 3.591, 5.111 and 5.632 nats.

**More edges can be worse.** `rook` -- every pair sharing a row or a column, 48
edges -- scores 4.694 and is beaten by a random graph holding six fewer.

## Why rook fails, which is the part worth keeping

`rook` is not an arbitrary choice. It is the graph you get by looking at the
data and connecting the cells that correlate: in the target, same-row and
same-column pairs have `<s_i s_j> = 0.467` and every other pair has -0.067.
Spend your connectivity where the correlation is, and you build `rook`.

Break the working graph down by edge type:

    type                     count   target <s_i s_j>   fitted |J|
    row/column adjacent         24             0.4667      23.8373
    diagonal                    18            -0.0667      15.8915

The 18 diagonals carry seven times less correlation than the edges the
heuristic would pick, and couplings of the same order. Delete them and you have
the `nearest neighbour` row: KL goes from 0.000 to 5.295.

The mechanism is local exclusion. In any valid configuration, every 2x2 block
has either both its rows equal or both its columns equal, and the blocks that
violate this are killed by an antiferromagnetic diagonal against a ferromagnetic
grid. The diagonal's job is to forbid a configuration, not to record an
association, and a term that forbids something occurring in neither phase leaves
almost no trace in the pairwise correlation it is measured by.

**Correlation is not the right allocation signal for connectivity.** The edges
that matter are the ones that exclude, and exclusion is close to invisible in
the statistic being exported.

## Priced against the hardware limit

Refitting the working graph under a box constraint on the couplings:

    |J| cap  KL(p||q)    valid  structured  moment err
       none     0.000   1.0000      0.9333     7.0e-08
        6.0     0.013   0.9873      0.9214     1.3e-03
        4.0     0.097   0.9074      0.8463     9.9e-03
        2.0     0.796   0.4531      0.4170     8.1e-02
        1.0     2.314   0.1044      0.0919     2.0e-01

A capped fit no longer matches the moments, and is not meant to. It is the best
a legal model can do, which is the question the compiler's `max_abs_coupling`
gate asks.

Z1's published cap is |J| <= 6.0, and on this workload it costs **0.013 nats**
out of 7.689, keeping 98.7 percent of the target's mass. Choosing the wrong 42
edges costs up to **5.632**.

    Z1's |J| <= 6.0 instead of unbounded couplings   0.013 nats
    the wrong 42 edges instead of the right 42       5.632 nats

Connectivity structure costs 433 times what the coupling cap costs. The cap is
the number people quote.

## What this does and does not establish

It establishes, exactly and without sampling, that for one target the graph a
moment readout is defined on determines whether it loses nothing or nearly
everything; that edge count and degree both fail to predict which; that the
correlation-following heuristic picks a graph beaten by chance; and that on this
workload the published coupling cap is a small cost next to that choice.

The mechanism generalises. The magnitudes do not. One target distribution, one
lattice size, one family of graphs, and bars-and-stripes was built out of local
constraints, which is exactly the structure a 2x2 exclusion can capture. A
target whose structure is genuinely long-range would rank these graphs
differently, and the 433 would be a different number or point the other way.

No energy claim. No hardware claim: `max_abs_coupling` is SOURCED in
`target.py`, and Z1's real behaviour under any of this is untested here.

## What follows for the compiler

The degree gate already refuses a model whose graph the hardware cannot hold.
Nothing yet tells a user that their graph, while legal, is the wrong shape for
what they are trying to represent -- and this result says that failure is
quieter and more expensive than the one the gate catches.

`passes.presolve` is the natural home: it already reads the declared contract,
which is where exclusion constraints are written down, and exclusion is
precisely what the correlation-following heuristic misses.
